# Weight Profile Rationale

This document explains **why** each weight profile emphasizes certain criteria. Understanding the rationale helps you use the profiles correctly and adapt them for new article types.

---

## Core Principle: Quality Drivers by Article Type

**Central idea:** Different article types address different research questions, so "quality" means different things.

| Article Type | Fundamental Question | Quality Driver | Therefore Weight... |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Empirical** | Is the result rigorously demonstrated? | Methodology rigor, statistical validity | Methods 30%, Results 15% |
| **Systematic Review** | Is evidence synthesis systematic & unbiased? | Comprehensive search, unbiased selection, synthesis quality | Search 15%, Methods 15%, Synthesis 15% |
| **Narrative Review** | Is this comprehensive, insightful, well-synthesized? | Coverage completeness, synthesis depth, organization | Literature 20%, Synthesis 15%, Structure 10% |
| **Theoretical** | Does this advance understanding conceptually? | Conceptual novelty, theoretical depth, logical consistency | Novelty 20%, Theory 18%, Consistency 12% |
| **Case Study** | Is case well-documented, analyzed, transferable? | Case significance, rigorous documentation, analytical insight | Uniqueness 15%, Rigor 15%, Quality 15% |

## Profile 1: Empirical / Experimental Research

### Fundamental Question
**"Is the result rigorously demonstrated?"**

### High-Weight Criteria Rationale

#### Criterion 7: Methodology (15%)
**Why so high?**
- In empirical research, the quality of evidence depends entirely on methodology
- Flawed design (e.g., lack of randomization, selection bias, inadequate controls) → findings unreliable regardless of statistical significance
- Reviewers must understand: sampling, randomization, blinding, control conditions
- **Example:** A study with huge effect size (p<.001) but no randomization is worth less than a study with moderate effect (p=.05) and rigorous RCT design

#### Criterion 9: Results (15%)
**Why so high?**
- After good methodology, the **reliability of findings** is paramount
- Results must be clearly presented, properly analyzed, confounds addressed
- Statistical significance ≠ clinical significance or reliability
- **Example:** "p=.04" with N=1000 might be trivial effect size; p=.07 with N=50 might be meaningful effect masked by low power
- Readers need effect sizes, confidence intervals, sensitivity analyses

#### Criterion 8: Statistics (8%)
**Why 8% (lower than methods/results)?**
- Statistics is *part of* methodology and results evaluation
- Weight 8% reflects: crucial for empirical work, but already partly captured in Methods (7%) and Results (9%)
- Not every empirical study needs advanced statistics (simple descriptive study with clear findings may score well)
- But when statistics are used, they must be correct

#### Criterion 2: Novelty (8%)
**Why 8% (moderate)?**
- Empirical novelty is important but secondary to rigor
- A perfectly executed replication study has high rigor but lower novelty
- Quality empirical research can be replication or direct investigation of new question
- **Principle:** A rigorous replication is better than a novel study with flawed methods

#### Criterion 22, 23: Ethics & Plagiarism (10% each)
**Why 10% each (high)?**
- These are **non-negotiable** for any research
- No amount of methodological rigor justifies unethical conduct or plagiarism
- Score ≤1 on either = automatic Reject (overrides overall score)

## Profile 2: Systematic Review / Meta-Analysis

### Fundamental Question
**"Is evidence synthesis systematic and unbiased?"**

### High-Weight Criteria Rationale

#### Criterion 6: Literature Search (15%)
**Why so high?**
- A systematic review is only as good as its literature search
- Biased, incomplete, or unreproducible search → findings unreliable
- Must search multiple databases, use rigorous inclusion/exclusion criteria, document process
- **Example:** Searching only PubMed (ignoring EMBASE, grey literature) → missed studies → biased synthesis
- **PRISMA requirement:** Complete search strategy (keywords, Boolean operators, date range) must be documented

#### Criterion 7: Systematic Review Methodology (15%)
**Why so high?**
- Selection bias is the critical risk in reviews
- How were studies chosen? Dual reviewers? Disagreement resolution? Inclusion/exclusion criteria explicit?
- **Example:** Author-selected studies (not blinded review) → selection for favorable results
- **PRISMA requirement:** PRISMA compliance, risk of bias assessment, GRADE evidence quality rating

#### Criterion 9: Evidence Synthesis Quality (15%)
**Why so high?**
- After finding all studies, synthesizing them properly is key
- Descriptive, critical synthesis (not just summarizing each study)
- **Example:** "Study A found X; Study B found Y; Study C found Z" (no synthesis) = poor; vs. "Studies show convergence on mechanism M, with exceptions explained by..." (synthesis) = good
- **Critical:** Quality appraisal of included studies, addressing heterogeneity, meta-analysis if appropriate

#### Criterion 8: Statistics (8%)
**Why 8% (lower)?**
- Meta-analysis is *optional* for systematic reviews
- Some reviews are descriptive syntheses (valid, but different evaluation)
- When meta-analysis done: heterogeneity assessment (I²), funnel plot, sensitivity analyses required
- **Principle:** Descriptive review done well (15% synthesis) ≥ meta-analysis done poorly

#### Criterion 2: Scientific Novelty (10%)
**Why 10% (moderate)?**
- Novelty in reviews = "Does this synthesis answer a new question or provide new insight?"
- Updating prior review can be highly valuable (captures recent literature)
- Novel organizing framework even for same-topic review is valuable
- **Principle:** A rigorous update of prior review is valuable; a novel question answered poorly is not

#### Criterion 12: Conclusions (8%)
**Why 8% (moderate)?**
- Conclusions should synthesize across studies, not just summarize each
- Should address implications for practice/research, gaps, future directions
- **Critical:** Avoid overgeneralizing ("This proves X works") when evidence is mixed or limited

## Profile 3: Narrative Literature Review

### Fundamental Question
**"Is this a comprehensive, insightful, well-synthesized review?"**

### High-Weight Criteria Rationale

#### Criterion 6: Literature Completeness (20%)
**Why 20% (highest)?**
- Narrative reviews don't use systematic protocol, so **coverage is critical**
- Readers trust author judgment on what's included/excluded
- **Good:** "We searched PubMed 2015–2025 using [terms]... and identified 120 papers; we selected 45 key papers based on [criteria]"
- **Bad:** "We reviewed papers we found relevant" (no documentation, potential bias)
- **Example:** Reviews of "critical thinking interventions" that miss half the literature are unreliable

#### Criterion 9: Critical Synthesis (15%)
**Why 15% (high)?**
- Readers want synthesis, not summary
- **Summary:** "Study A found X; Study B found Y" (unhelpful)
- **Synthesis:** "Most studies (70%) find X under conditions C1, but Study B finds Y under conditions C2, suggesting mechanism M. Future research should..."
- Synthesis requires critical evaluation, not just aggregation

#### Criterion 4: Theoretical Framing (15%)
**Why 15% (high)?**
- Narrative reviews can **add value through organizing framework**
- Example: Review could organize 50 studies of "teacher effectiveness" into frameworks: (1) content knowledge, (2) pedagogical skills, (3) classroom climate, (4) assessment practices
- Framework adds value beyond what individual papers provide

#### Criterion 12: Conclusions (10%)
**Why 10% (moderate)?**
- Conclusions should synthesize across themes, identify gaps, suggest future research
- **Good:** "Literature converges on X, but underexplores Y, suggesting future research should..." 
- **Bad:** Conclusions just repeat main finding from each study

#### Criterion 2: Novelty (12%)
**Why 12%?**
- Novelty = new synthesis, new framework, new insight (not necessarily new empirical finding)
- **Example:** 
  - Novelty ✅: "This review is first to organize [topic] using [novel framework]"
  - No novelty ❌: "Another review of the same topic, nothing new"
- Reviews updating prior reviews can still be novel if new findings/framework

#### Criterion 17: Logical Structure (10%)
**Why 10%?**
- Narrative reviews must be well-organized to be useful
- **Good structure:** Clear section organization, themes build on each other, reader can follow logic
- **Bad structure:** Random assortment of studies; reader can't see connections

#### Note: Criteria 7, 8 marked N/A
- Narrative reviews don't report original empirical methods or statistics
- (Unless review incorporates empirical data collection—then these would apply)

## Profile 4: Theoretical / Conceptual Paper

### Fundamental Question
**"Does this advance understanding conceptually?"**

### High-Weight Criteria Rationale

#### Criterion 2: Scientific Novelty (20%)
**Why 20% (highest)?**
- Theoretical papers are *only valuable if novel*
- **Novel:** "This theory explains X in a new way" or "This framework unifies previously separate concepts"
- **Not novel:** "This paper explains existing theory" (just review material)
- **Example:** 
  - Novelty ✅: Lakoff & Johnson's "Conceptual Metaphor Theory" (new way of understanding language)
  - No novelty ❌: "An explanation of how memory works" (if just summarizing existing literature)

#### Criterion 4: Theoretical Significance (18%)
**Why 18% (very high)?**
- Does this theory **matter**? 
- Does it explain important phenomena?
- Does it open new research directions?
- **Example:**
  - Significant ✅: Theory explains why some educational interventions transfer to new contexts while others don't (has implications)
  - Trivial ❌: Theory explains a narrow, low-impact phenomenon

#### Criterion 3: Clarity of Concepts (15%)
**Why 15% (high)?**
- Theoretical papers must define concepts precisely
- If reader can't understand core concepts, theory is useless
- **Example:**
  - Clear ✅: "We define 'adaptive expertise' as the ability to modify knowledge and procedures in novel contexts (contrasts with 'routine expertise')"
  - Vague ❌: "This paper explores the concept of 'deep learning'" (undefined)

#### Criterion 12: Logical Consistency (12%)
**Why 12%?**
- Do premises lead to conclusions?
- Are there internal contradictions?
- **Example:**
  - Consistent ✅: Theory posits A→B and B→C, therefore A→C (conclusion follows)
  - Inconsistent ❌: Theory depends on "all humans are rational" but acknowledges cognitive biases (contradiction)

#### Criterion 1: Problem Relevance (10%)
**Why 10%?**
- Is the problem worth theorizing about?
- Does theory address real gap or open question?

#### Criterion 17: Logical Structure / Argument Flow (10%)
**Why 10%?**
- Does argument build clearly from premises to conclusions?
- Can reader follow reasoning?

#### Note: Criteria 7, 8, 9 marked N/A
- Theoretical papers don't report empirical methods, statistics, or reliability of data
- (Unless paper includes empirical validation—then these would apply)

## Profile 5: Case Study / Descriptive Report

### Fundamental Question
**"Is case well-documented, analyzed, and transferable?"**

### High-Weight Criteria Rationale

#### Criterion 7: Data Collection Rigor (15%)
**Why 15% (high)?**
- Case study value depends on **quality of documentation**
- Were multiple data sources used? Systematic observation? Interviews conducted properly?
- **Example:**
  - Rigorous ✅: "We conducted 20 semi-structured interviews (audio-recorded, transcribed), reviewed 50 institutional documents, and made 10 site visits (field notes)"
  - Weak ❌: "We interviewed the director and reviewed their reports"

#### Criterion 9: Data Quality (15%)
**Why 15% (high)?**
- Is the evidence sufficient to support claims?
- Do findings emerge from data or are they imposed on data?
- **Example:**
  - High quality ✅: "Claim X supported by: (1) multiple interviews mentioning X, (2) archival documents referencing X, (3) observational field notes documenting X"
  - Low quality ❌: "We found X" (no evidence cited)

#### Criterion 2: Case Uniqueness / Significance (15%)
**Why 15% (high)?**
- Why is *this particular case* worth studying?
- **Example:**
  - Unique ✅: "This case is the first school to implement [model] in [context], so lessons are instructive"
  - Generic ❌: "This is a typical school like any other"

#### Criterion 5: Transferability & Applicability (12%)
**Why 12%?**
- Case studies don't generalize statistically, but findings should be **transferable** to similar contexts
- Author should help readers assess: "Could lessons from this case apply to my context?"
- **Example:**
  - Transferable ✅: "Context includes [demographics], [resources], [constraints]; readers in similar contexts can assess applicability"
  - Non-transferable ❌: Context vague; readers can't assess whether findings apply elsewhere

#### Criterion 1: Case Relevance (12%)
**Why 12%?**
- Is this case addressing a relevant question or illustrating important phenomenon?

#### Criterion 12: Conclusions & Generalizability Awareness (10%)
**Why 10%?**
- Do conclusions avoid overgeneralization?
- Are limitations of single-case (vs. multi-case) acknowledged?
- **Example:**
  - Good ✅: "This case illustrates mechanism M; multi-case study would test whether M generalizes"
  - Bad ❌: "This case proves X" (overgeneralization from single case)

#### Criterion 17: Narrative Clarity (8%)
**Why 8%?**
- Case study story must be told clearly; readers must understand context and findings

## Adaptation for New Article Types

### Process: Creating a Weight Profile for New Type

1. **Define the central question:** "What makes a [new type] paper high-quality?"
2. **Identify quality drivers:** What criteria matter most for answering that question?
3. **Assign weights:**
   - High weight (15–20%): Critical quality drivers
   - Moderate weight (8–12%): Important but secondary
   - Low/0% weight: Irrelevant for this type
4. **Sum to 100%**
5. **Document rationale:** Why each weight?

### Example: Creating a Profile for "Rapid Communication"

**Central question:** "Does this study present preliminary findings quickly without full methodology?"

**Quality drivers:**
- Novelty: Very high (only worth publishing if very new finding) → 20%
- Methodology: Lower (can be preliminary) → 5% (or N/A)
- Results clarity: High (must be clear despite preliminary nature) → 12%
- Limitations: High (must acknowledge preliminary limitations) → 15%
- Ethics: High (even preliminary studies need ethics) → 10%
- Plagiarism: High → 10%
- (Others): 28%

**Total:** 100%

This profile emphasizes novelty, transparency about limitations, and rapid dissemination, while de-emphasizing full methodological rigor (since "rapid" implies preliminary).
